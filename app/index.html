Category Discovery Engine + First Buy

What 3,000 reviews say about why people never leave their aisle

A five-stage pipeline that reads public Zepto reviews, classifies each one against a closed barrier taxonomy, and produces themes where every claim traces back to a quote you can check. Built for the question: why do high-frequency users stop entering new categories?

The pipeline

STAGE 1
Ingest
Google Play + App Store reviews, deduped, unified schema, provenance logged.
python / no LLM
STAGE 2
Relevance gate
Binary screen, tuned for recall. Roughly 9 in 10 reviews are about delivery or price and carry no discovery signal.
haiku · 5 output tokens
STAGE 3
Structured extract
JTBD, categories, barrier code, competitor, workaround, verbatim, self-reported confidence.
sonnet · strict JSON
STAGE 4
Aggregate
Frequency, severity from star rating, category×barrier co-occurrence.
python / deterministic
STAGE 5
Synthesise
Themes ranked by strategic weight, each carrying review IDs and a stated confidence level.
sonnet
STAGE 6
Validate
60-review blind hold-out, hand-labelled. Precision, recall, Cohen's kappa, confusion matrix.
human + python
Why the gate is a separate cheap stage. It cuts extraction spend by roughly 90% at almost no recall cost, and it makes the funnel legible: you can see exactly how many reviews were discarded and why. The gate fails open — on genuine uncertainty it returns YES, because a false positive costs one API call and a false negative loses a signal permanently. Asymmetric error, asymmetric threshold.

First Buy: a deliberation layer, in the cart, because that is where users said yes

  
Interviews located the barrier precisely: the product page cannot answer the question users
  need answered before entering a new category — no verified reviews, no real photos, no specs, no seller
  verification. First Buy puts that answer at the moment two of six users had already converted through.

1 · The surface: the cart — and six interviews are why it moved there

          
I originally designed this for the post-payment tracking
          screen: basket already converted, zero cannibalisation risk, user idle for ten minutes. It was a
          tidy argument and no user supported it.

          
Two of six had already entered a new category through a
          checkout prompt. One asked for contextual cart bundling by name. One skips home banners entirely.
          Nobody expanded by browsing, and nobody described anything resembling a post-delivery moment. So the
          card moved to the cart, and the cannibalisation risk I had designed around is now the hardest
          guardrail rather than a solved problem.

        

        

          
2 · The trigger: an observation, not a recommendation

          
Not "you might like." A specific, checkable statement about the
          user's own behaviour: "You've ordered milk and cereal 21 times. You've never ordered fruit."
          Specificity is what makes it read as the app noticing rather than the app advertising. It also
          makes the suggestion falsifiable, which is why it earns attention.

        

        

          
3 · One question, then one answer

          
Not an open chat box — blank inputs in a task-mode app get
          ignored. One question that resolves the single largest uncertainty in the category, then exactly one
          product: price, one reason, and the top objection pre-handled. Not a grid. A grid is what the user
          was already failing at.

        

        

          
4 · The First Buy promise

          
Free return on the first purchase in any new category, plus a
          trial size where supply allows. This attacks price-risk structurally instead of with a discount —
          cheaper per conversion, and it doesn't train users to wait for coupons.

        

      


      

        

          
The same corpus does both jobs

          
This is the part that makes it AI-native rather than AI-flavoured. Ranking is not
          the bottleneck — collaborative filtering has been good at "what would this user like" for fifteen
          years. The bottleneck is justification: producing a specific, trustworthy reason at the exact
          moment of hesitation. That is a generative problem, and it is fed by the same review mining that
          diagnosed the barrier.

What I'd flag to my own team

          
    
            
  • A cart card can cost you the order. Moving to the cart follows the evidence but raises the stakes: anything that delays checkout risks the basket that was already converting. Cart-to-order conversion is the hardest guardrail, and I'd kill the feature over it.
    • 
            
  • Reverse logistics is the expensive half. Free returns is a one-line policy change and a genuinely hard operational programme on a 10-minute network. The policy is not the work.
    • 
            
  • Hallucination is a category risk, not a quality risk. Constrained generation over a verified retrieval set, plus a blocklist on health and safety claims.
    • 
            
  • Blinkit can ship the same surface in a quarter. The UI isn't defensible. The corpus and the reason-generation quality compound; the card layout doesn't.
    • 
            
  • Regulatory context. Zepto already carries a CCPA dark-patterns notice on basket sneaking, and the cart is exactly where that risk lives. The line is consent: never pre-selected, never auto-added, visually separate from the basket, dismissible in one tap. Basket sneaking is adding without asking. This asks.
    • 
          

        

      

    

  







  
How I know the machine wasn't just confidently wrong

  
An LLM will label 3,000 reviews and hand you a clean chart whether or not the labels mean
  anything. The chart looks identical either way. So the labels get scored against a human.


  


  
The protocol

  

    
    
      
  1. Stratified hold-out. 60 reviews, fixed seed, sampled by model label so rare barriers actually appear. Unstratified sampling from a skewed distribution gives you 55 rows of the majority class and tells you nothing about the classes that matter.
    2. 
      
  2. Blind hand-labelling. Labelled by me against the same closed taxonomy, before looking at any model output. Not blind means not evidence.
    3. 
      
  3. Score. Per-barrier precision, recall and F1 — not accuracy alone, which is inflated by the majority class.
    4. 
      
  4. Cohen's kappa. Agreement corrected for chance. With 7 classes, raw agreement flatters; kappa doesn't.
    5. 
      
  5. Confusion matrix. Reported in full. Knowing which pair the model confuses is more useful than the headline number — a Trust/Fit confusion, for instance, wouldn't change the strategic conclusion, because both are deliberation barriers.
    6. 
      
  6. Negative control. Gate-rejected reviews checked for leakage, confirming the model isn't manufacturing barriers where none exist.
    7. 
      
  7. Triangulation. Every top theme is put to users in interviews as a question I could lose. Reviews are a biased sample; interviews are the check.
    8. 
    

  


  
What this data cannot tell me

  

    
    
      
  • App-store reviews are a complaint channel. People who are quietly satisfied and quietly narrow don't write reviews. The silent majority of Plateaued Regulars is invisible here — which is exactly why the interviews recruit for behaviour, not for opinions.
    • 
      
  • No behavioural ground truth. Reviews say what people report; they don't say what people did. Every claim here would be re-tested against internal cohort data on day one.
    • 
      
  • English-language skew. Hindi and regional-language reviews are under-represented by the gate. A named limitation, not a solved one.
    • 
      
  • Self-selection by intensity. Reviews over-represent strong feeling in both directions. Frequency counts here indicate salience, not prevalence — I use them to rank hypotheses, not to size populations.
