# User Research Kit — 6 interviews, one day

I can build you everything else. I cannot do this part, and you shouldn't want me to. Fabricated
interview data is the single fastest way to lose this — evaluators who read a lot of these decks can
spot invented quotes, because real people are messier and less on-message than invented ones. Six real
30-minute phone calls will beat any synthetic transcript, and they'll give you the one thing nothing
else can: a moment where a user contradicts your thesis and you have to think.

**Time cost: ~4 hours total.** 6 × 30 min calls + 60 min synthesis. Phone is fine. Video is not required.

---

## 1. Who to recruit — screen for behaviour, not opinions

You want **Plateaued Regulars**. Screen with these four questions. They must pass all four.

1. "Roughly how many times a month do you order on Zepto/Blinkit/Instamart?" → **need 5+**
2. "How long have you been using it?" → **need 12+ months**
3. "Off the top of your head, what do you usually order?" → **listen for a narrow, repeating list**
4. "When did you last buy something on it from a category you'd never bought before?" → **need "can't remember" or "months ago"**

If someone says "oh I buy everything on there, electronics, clothes, everything" — thank them and
**recruit them anyway as a contrast case.** One broad explorer in a sample of six is worth more than a
sixth plateaued user, because they tell you what unlocking looks like. Aim for 5 + 1.

**Where to find them fast:** your own WhatsApp contacts, your building's residents group, college
groups, your parents' friends, colleagues. You need people who order groceries on an app and live in a
metro. That is most urban Indians under 45.

Mix it up: don't do six people your own age in your own city. At minimum vary age (25–45), household
type (alone / couple / family with kids), and city if you can. Note the composition honestly in the
deck. A skewed sample you've named is fine. A skewed sample you've hidden is not.

---

## 2. The recruiting message

> Hi ___ — I'm doing a product research project on grocery delivery apps for a fellowship. Could I
> borrow 25 minutes on a call this weekend? No selling anything, nothing technical — I just want to hear
> how you actually shop on Zepto/Blinkit. Would tomorrow around ___ work?

That's it. Don't over-explain. Don't say "I want to know why you don't buy new categories" — you've just
told them the answer you want and they'll give it to you.

---

## 3. The interview guide

**Ground rules for you, not them:**

- **Ask about the last time, never about "usually."** "Usually" gets you a self-image. "Last Tuesday"
  gets you a behaviour.
- **Shut up.** After they answer, count to three before speaking. The second half of the answer is the
  useful half, and it only arrives if you leave the silence there.
- **Never say the word "would."** "Would you use a feature that…" produces polite fiction. People are
  extraordinarily bad at predicting their own behaviour and extraordinarily good at describing what they
  did last week.
- **Chase the workaround.** Every time they describe going somewhere else, slow down and get the full
  sequence. The workaround is where the whole insight lives.

---

### Opening — 3 min

> Thanks for doing this. There are no right answers, I'm not testing you, and if you think a question is
> stupid please say so. Can I record just for my notes? It won't go anywhere else.

> To start — can you open Zepto on your phone right now and go to your order history?

*(This is the highest-leverage 30 seconds of the whole interview. You get real data instead of memory.)*

---

### Section A — Map the actual repertoire (8 min)

1. Read me the last five orders. Just the items.
2. How much of that list is basically the same every time?
3. When you open the app, do you know what you're getting before you open it, or do you decide inside?
4. Walk me through the last order start to finish. Where were you, what were you doing, how long did it take?
5. Scroll to the bottom of the home page for me. Tell me what you see. *(They usually have never scrolled that far. Watch what happens.)*

**Listening for:** session length, whether they browse at all, whether they can even name categories
they've never opened.

---

### Section B — The last new thing (8 min)

6. When's the last time you bought something on there that you'd never bought before?
7. Take me back to that. What made you try it that time?
8. Did you buy it again?
9. *(If no)* What happened?

10. Now the opposite — think of something you buy regularly that you've **never** bought on Zepto, even
    though they sell it. What is it?
11. Where do you buy it instead?
12. Walk me through the last time you bought it. Start from the moment you realised you needed it.
13. What has to be true before you'd buy that on Zepto?

**Question 13 is the whole interview.** Everything before it is setup. Do not rush it, and do not accept
the first answer — the first answer is usually "if it was cheaper," which is almost never the real
reason. Ask "and if the price were identical?"

---

### Section C — The uncertainty moment (8 min)

14. Last time you bought something you weren't sure about — any app, any shop — what did you do before buying?
15. What were you actually trying to find out?
16. Who or what do you trust when you don't know which product to pick?
17. If Zepto showed you a product in a category you'd never bought, what's the first thing you'd want to know about it?
18. Has an app ever recommended something to you that you actually bought? What was different about that one?

**Listening for:** the specific *question* they need answered. That question is the product spec.

---

### Section D — Reaction, last (3 min)

Only now, at the end, describe First Buy. Not before — you'll contaminate everything above.

> Imagine after you place an order, while you're waiting for the delivery, the app says: "you've bought
> cereal and milk 21 times, you've never bought fruit" — asks you one question, then shows you one
> product with a reason, and free return if you don't like it.

19. What's your honest first reaction?
20. What's wrong with it?
21. When in your day would that be most annoying?

**Push for the negative.** Ask "what's wrong with it" before "do you like it." People are polite; you
have to give them permission. A deck that reports "4 of 6 said the tracking screen is where they're
least likely to look" is dramatically more credible than one where everyone loved it.

---

### Close

22. Anything I should have asked and didn't?

**Fill in at least one "Challenged."** If everything confirms, you either got lucky, ran a leading
interview, or aren't looking hard enough — and an evaluator will assume the second. A submission that
says *"the engine over-weighted trust; three of six users described a fit problem they'd initially
worded as a trust problem"* demonstrates more capability than one where the machine was right about
everything.

## 5. What to actually put in the deck

- **n=6, and say n=6.** Don't dress it up. Say "6 interviews, recruited on a behavioural screen, 5
  plateaued + 1 contrast case." Naming the sample size and the recruiting logic reads as rigour.
- **Verbatims, not paraphrases.** Two or three short quotes beat a summary paragraph.
- **Never percentages on n=6.** "4 of 6" — never "67%." Percentages on six people is the tell of someone
  who doesn't understand sample sizes.
- **One thing that surprised you.** Lead with it if it's good.
- **Consent.** First names or "P3" only. Say in the appendix that participants consented to anonymised
  quotes.

---

## 6. If you genuinely run out of time

Do **four**, not zero, and label it "4 interviews, n=4, a limitation I'd close before building." Four
real interviews with an honest caveat scores far better than six fabricated ones, and infinitely better
than skipping the section — Part 2 is a graded requirement and a hole there is a guaranteed loss.
